# How to Make a Winning YC-Level Seed Pitch Deck

*A complete playbook distilled from Y Combinator guidance, Michael Seibel's advice, and analysis of decks from Airbnb, Dropbox, Buffer, Coinbase, DoorDash, Brex, and others — plus lessons learned building the Crayon deck.*

---

## Part 1: The Mindset (Before You Touch a Single Slide)

### The 60-Second Rule

Michael Seibel, Managing Director at YC, is clear about this: **you earn every two minutes of an investor's meeting.** Investors spend an average of 3 minutes and 44 seconds on a deck. If you don't hook them in the first 60 seconds, they're mentally checked out.

This means your first 2-3 slides carry 80% of the weight. Lead with whatever is most impressive — not what comes first in a template.

### The #1 Mistake Founders Make

Seibel says the biggest barrier to getting funded isn't your product or your market — **it's you.** Specifically:

- If investors don't understand what your company does in 2 sentences, they can't fund you
- Jargon signals insecurity, not expertise
- Better to be 80% accurate and 100% clear than vice versa
- Investors aren't your customers — they don't need the technical explanation

### What Investors Are Really Deciding

Every investor sits in one of three mindsets during your pitch:

1. **Already decided no** — nothing you say will change this
2. **Already decided yes** — you just need to not screw it up
3. **On the fence** — this is your biggest opportunity

For group 3, forcing a direct yes or no creates psychological discomfort that often results in checks being written. **70% of YC founders don't even ask for money.** Don't be one of them.

## Part 2: The Structure (10-12 Slides, No More)

YC's format works because it's brutally efficient. Here's the slide-by-slide breakdown with what to include and what to avoid.

### Slide 1: Title

**Job:** Tell them what you do in one breath.

Your company name + a one-liner that a 5-year-old could understand. That's it.

**Real example — Airbnb:** "Book rooms with locals, not hotels."
Six words. Instantly clear. No jargon.

**Real example — Coinbase (2012):** Their seed deck was 12 slides and the title made it immediately clear they were building the easiest way to buy and sell bitcoin — when BTC was $6.25.

**Real example — Crayon (our deck):** "We find, research, and enrich your buyers — so all you do is close."

**Rules:**
- One-liner must pass the "would my mom understand this?" test
- No taglines that could apply to any company ("Revolutionizing the future of work")
- Include "Seed Round · [Year] · Confidential" at the bottom

### Slide 2: Problem

**Job:** Make the investor feel the pain in 5 seconds.

This is the most important slide after the title. If investors don't believe the problem is real, nothing else matters.

**Real example — Airbnb:** Three short, front-loaded sentences:
- **Price** is an important concern for customers booking travel online
- **Hotels** leave you disconnected from the city and its culture
- **No easy way** exists to book a room with a local

Notice: bold keywords at the start of each line. You can skim-read it in 3 seconds.

**Real example — Dropbox (2007):** "Storage is a mess." That's essentially the entire problem statement. Then they showed the pain points everyone recognized — emailing files to yourself, USB drives, broken sync.

**Real example — Crayon (our deck):** We used two problem slides:
1. First slide: "Your sales team spends more time on tools than on selling" + scattered logos of 20 GTM SaaS tools (Clay, Instantly, Apollo, ZoomInfo, etc.) floating freely — creating a visual feeling of tool overload. Bottom line: *"The market has tools. It doesn't have a system."*
2. Second slide: Direct comparison of what Clay does well vs. what it can't do — positioning Clay as the $1.3B incumbent that proved the model but still needs a human operator.

**What to avoid:**
- Don't describe the problem in abstract terms. Be specific.
- Don't use data without context. "73% of SDRs say..." means nothing without the "so what."
- Don't name competitors on every slide — save it for Problem and Competition only.

### Slide 3: Solution

**Job:** Show how you solve the problem in plain English.

Not how the technology works. Not the architecture. **What happens for the user.**

**Real example — Airbnb:** "A web platform where users can rent out their space." Then three value props: saving money on travel, making money listing space, and local cultural exchange. Simple.

**Real example — Buffer:** Described what Buffer does in one sentence, then showed the interface. No technical explanation of their API or scheduling algorithm.

**Real example — Crayon:** "Describe your ideal customer. Crayon does the rest." Then showed the flow: ICP Discovery → Multi-Source Research → Signal Detection → Social Listening — with enriched contacts flowing to sequencers via API.

**Rules:**
- 3 bullet points max
- Each bullet = one outcome for the user
- If you need a paragraph to explain your solution, you don't understand it well enough yet

### Slide 4: Why Now

**Job:** Create urgency. Explain why this moment — not 2 years ago, not 2 years from now — is the right time.

**Real example — DoorDash (2013):** They opened with the fact that 70% of the U.S. lacked food delivery options. The infrastructure (smartphones, GPS, mobile payments) had just reached critical mass. The market was ready but unserved.

**What makes a strong "Why Now":**
- A technology shift (AI models now good enough for X)
- A market shift (buyers now expect Y)
- A regulatory or behavioral change
- A cost shift (something that was expensive is now cheap)

**Format:** 3 numbered points. Each one sentence + one supporting line.

### Slide 5: Market Size

**Job:** Prove the opportunity is big enough to return a fund.

Use a concentric circle (TAM → SAM → SOM) diagram. Show your math.

**Real example — Airbnb:** Originally had their market in millions. Sam Altman told them investors wanted to see billions. They changed the "m" to a "b." The lesson: investors need to see a path to a massive outcome.

**Real example — Coinbase:** Showed the total cryptocurrency market + transaction volume as TAM, then narrowed to their serviceable wedge.

**Real example — Crayon (our deck):** Three concentric circles: TAM $78B (entire sales tech), SAM $12B (enrichment + intent + automation), SOM $500M (mid-market teams using Clay-like tools). Bottom line anchored to Clay's $1.3B valuation.

**Michael Seibel's advice:** Calculate market size with transparent math. Show your work. Bottom-up is more credible than top-down. "There are X potential customers × Y average deal size = Z total market" beats "Gartner says the market is $50B."

### Slide 6: Product

**Job:** Show what the product actually does (not screenshots unless they're polished).

**Real example — Buffer:** Showed actual product screenshots because their UI was clean and self-explanatory.

**Real example — Dropbox:** Famously used a demo video instead of screenshots because the product was hard to explain visually. The video went viral.

**If your product isn't built yet:** Describe features as agents, modules, or capabilities — each with a one-line description and an icon. This is what we did for Crayon (4 autonomous agents, each with a clear job).

**Rules:**
- One idea per feature
- No technical architecture diagrams
- If showing UI, make sure it looks polished — better to not show it than show something rough

### Slide 7: Traction

**Job:** Prove momentum, not just metrics.

**Michael Seibel's critical insight:** The biggest mistake YC founders make is **not putting a time frame on their traction.** Without time, nothing is impressive.

- "100 users" = meaningless
- "100 users in 2 weeks with zero marketing spend" = very impressive
- "100 users in 2 years" = concerning

**Real example — DoorDash:** Led with 31% week-over-week growth in deliveries. The growth rate told the story — they were accelerating.

**Real example — Buffer:** Showed MRR growth, number of paying customers, and CAC — classic SaaS metrics presented clearly. Buffer is the gold standard for seed-stage SaaS traction slides.

**Real example — Crayon (our deck):** Pre-product, so we structured traction across three ventures: B2B Wizards agency (300+ clients, $50M+ pipeline), WizLeads.io SaaS (200+ monthly signups, LinkedIn scraping tool), and then Crayon as the full vision. Each venture stood as independent proof of capability.

**Seibel's other rules for traction:**
- Don't share work that isn't furthering the company
- A bad traction slide is worse than no traction slide
- If you don't have traction, skip this slide entirely

### Slide 8: Competition

**Job:** Show you understand the landscape and why you win.

**Real example — Dropbox:** Used a simple tick-and-cross grid showing Dropbox vs. competitors across key features. Instantly visual. No paragraphs.

**Real example — Airbnb:** Acknowledged competitors exist but showed clear differentiation — Airbnb wasn't competing with hotels on amenities, it was competing on experience and price.

**Real example — Crayon (our deck):** Direct "Clay vs Crayon" head-to-head table. 6 features with ✗ (Clay) and ✓ (Crayon) icons. Clay column in muted gray, Crayon column in bold coral. Bottom line: *"They built the spreadsheet. We're building the operating system."*

**What NOT to do:**
- Don't claim you have no competitors. Investors see this as naivety.
- Don't bash competitors. Give them credit, then show where they fall short.
- Don't use a 2x2 magic quadrant — investors see right through it.

### Slide 9: Team

**Job:** Prove you're the right people to build this.

**Michael Seibel's advice:** Share credentials and accomplishments, not titles. "Built infrastructure handling $2B+/month in live trades" is 100x more powerful than "Senior Software Engineer at Goldman Sachs."

Weave how you've experienced the problem into the team slide — investors will think of you as an expert.

**Real example — Airbnb:** The founders showed they had personally experienced the problem (couldn't afford rent, started renting airbeds). Domain expertise through lived experience.

**Real example — Crayon (our deck):** Two side-by-side cards: Vatsal (CEO) — built GTM for 300+ companies, $50M+ in pipeline; Pranav (CTO) — built algo trading infra processing $2B+/month, zero downtime in 3+ years. Circular headshots, LinkedIn hyperlinks, 4 bullet points each with numbers. Headline: "Operators who've lived inside the problem."

**Rules:**
- Real photos (circular crop, professional)
- 3-4 bullets each with NUMBERS
- LinkedIn hyperlinks for credibility
- If you have relevant angel investors or advisors, mention them

### Slide 10: The Ask

**Job:** Tell them exactly how much, how it'll be used, and what it buys.

**Seibel's framework:** "We're raising $X to reach [specific milestone] within [timeframe]."

**Example:** "We're raising $1.5M to reach $100K MRR within 18 months by expanding from 5 enterprise customers to 50."

**What to include:**
- Raise amount (large, bold)
- Use of funds (3-4 categories with percentages — engineering, GTM, ops)
- Key milestones at 6mo, 12mo, 18mo, 24mo
- What the next raise looks like (Series A triggers)

**What to avoid:**
- Don't leave out the ask — this is where 70% of founders fail
- Don't be vague: "We need funding to grow" tells investors nothing
- Don't ask for an amount that doesn't match your plan

### Slide 11: Closing

**Job:** Leave one sentence ringing in their ears.

Company name, final tagline, contact email. Same aesthetic as the title slide — bookend the deck.

## Part 3: Design Principles (What Separates Good from Great)

### YC's Three Laws of Deck Design

1. **Legibility:** Large, bold fonts with high contrast. Readable from the back of a room.
2. **Simplicity:** One idea per slide. No clutter, no excessive branding.
3. **Obviousness:** If someone unfamiliar with your startup can't understand a slide in seconds, simplify it.

### Michael Seibel's Controversial Take

"If you need a designer to build a slide deck, you're doing it wrong. Your designer has no idea what the most important points of the pitch are, so they may emphasize the wrong things."

This doesn't mean your deck should look ugly — it means **design should serve clarity, not compete with it.** The best seed decks are visually clean, not visually stunning.

### What We Learned Building the Crayon Deck

- **Warm/dark backgrounds beat white** — they feel more premium and are easier on the eyes during long pitch sessions
- **Serif headlines + sans-serif body** gives editorial authority without feeling corporate
- **One bottom-of-slide italic tagline** per slide lands the point and gives investors a takeaway
- **Scattered logos > grid logos** for landscape slides — creates the "overwhelm" feeling you want
- **Head-to-head comparison tables** with ✓/✗ icons are the clearest way to show competitive advantage
- **Real photos with LinkedIn links** for the team slide — investors will check your profiles

## Part 4: The Non-Obvious Rules (What No One Tells You)

### 1. Your Pitch Is a Conversation, Not a Presentation

Seibel: "Don't treat a pitch like delivering a book report. Ideally, your pitch should be a way to draw investors into conversation. They're convincing themselves to invest, so the more they can talk, the more they can talk themselves into giving you money."

### 2. Lead with Your Best, Not Your First

If your traction is your strongest point, put it on slide 2. If your team is legendary, lead with that. The YC template is a guideline, not a prison.

### 3. Non-Obvious Insights Win

Every investor has seen "the market is big" and "incumbents are slow." What do you know that others don't? What did you learn from doing this manually that no one building from a whiteboard would know?

For Crayon, the non-obvious insight was: "We've been running Crayon manually for 6 years through our agency. Every workflow we automate, we've done 10,000 times by hand."

### 4. Time Makes Traction Impressive

"We have 200 users" = boring. "We launched 3 weeks ago, zero ad spend, 200 organic signups" = impressive. Always include the timeframe.

### 5. The Deck Is the Beginning, Not the End

The best decks open doors to conversations. The conversation is where the deal happens. As Seibel notes, if the investor is talking more than 50% of the time, you're winning — they're selling themselves on the deal.

## Part 5: Quick Reference Checklist

Before you send your deck, check every box:

**Content:**
- [ ] Can someone understand what you do from slide 1 alone?
- [ ] Is the problem specific, not abstract?
- [ ] Does your solution describe outcomes, not technology?
- [ ] Does your traction include timeframes?
- [ ] Is your market sized bottom-up, not just top-down?
- [ ] Do you directly ask for money with a specific number?
- [ ] Are milestones tied to the raise amount?

**Design:**
- [ ] One idea per slide?
- [ ] Large fonts, high contrast?
- [ ] No slide requires more than 5 seconds to understand?
- [ ] No paragraphs on any slide?
- [ ] Real founder photos, not placeholders?
- [ ] Clean, minimal aesthetic that serves the story?

**Delivery:**
- [ ] Can you explain each slide in under 30 seconds?
- [ ] Do you know which 2-3 slides are your strongest?
- [ ] Are those strong slides near the front?
- [ ] Have you practiced with someone who knows nothing about your industry?
